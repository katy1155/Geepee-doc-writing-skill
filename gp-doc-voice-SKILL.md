# GP Doc Voice

Writing rules for Katy's NID Graduation Project document, the landscape A3 book documenting her JioHotstar internship. These were established over several rounds of editing and should hold for every future draft in this document, not just the section they came from.

## Run avoid-ai-writing and stop-slop first, every time

Apply both skills before delivering any draft. No em dashes. No adverbs. No passive voice on inanimate subjects ("the complaint becomes a fix"). No "not X, it's Y" contrasts. No fragment lists as paragraph closers. No pull-quote lines.

## Never reuse these opening patterns

Flagged as filler, already cut once, must not resurface in any form:

- "It still left one question unanswered:"
- "Stated formally at this point,"
- "Underneath that goal sat a hypothesis worth stating plainly,"
- "The research surfaced/showed/made clear one [thing]:" (e.g. "The research made one thing clear:" / "The research surfaced one clear conclusion.")
- "[X] worked for what it was built for" / "[X] did what it was designed to do" — a tautological opener that restates the mechanism's existence before saying anything about what it actually did. When describing what worked and what didn't, start directly with the concrete case (the actual event, the actual limitation), not a meta-statement that the thing succeeded at its own purpose.

The pattern underneath all of these: a sentence that announces its own function ("here's the question," "to put it plainly," "worth stating," "it worked for what it was built for") before delivering the actual content. Any opener that names what it's about to do, instead of just doing it, is this same tell in a new sentence. Cut the announcement, keep the sentence that follows it.

## Never close on a symmetric "what X was for, what Y was for" line

Closing a paragraph by restating two things in parallel purpose-clauses ("that was the bar X set, and Y was the test of it" / "for this reason, and for that reason") reads as a tidy résumé line rather than a real ending. Close on the concrete last fact instead, a name, an action, a plain outcome, not a symmetrical summary of purposes.

When a new instance of this pattern gets called out in conversation, add it to this list so it doesn't return later in the document.

## The not-X-but-Y contrast, in any disguise

Already banned via avoid-ai-writing/stop-slop, but it keeps resurfacing wearing different words, so name it explicitly: any sentence pair where the first half negates an action and the second half affirms a different one, even without the literal words "not" or "it's."

- Flagged example: "Vivek and Ashleysha didn't ask about the visuals. They asked about the edges."
- Same structure as: "It's not X, it's Y" / "This isn't about X, it's about Y" / "wasn't asking X, was asking Y"

Check for this at the structural level, negate-then-affirm across two clauses or sentences, not just by scanning for the words "not" and "but." If a sentence is doing this with any vocabulary, rewrite it as one direct statement instead.

## Never reuse these phrases, anywhere in the sentence

Flagged as tics, not just openers, so check the whole sentence, not just the first few words:

- "earns its place"
- "before [X] touches it" (e.g. "before any theme touches it")
- "nobody asked [it/them] to [verb]" (e.g. "attention nobody asked it to draw")
- "just stating it"
- "with real [X] behind [them/it]" (e.g. "walked into the review with real structure behind them")
- an abstract noun turned into an agent that "can hold/carry/do" something it's "never seen before" (e.g. "A bucket built from intent can hold something it's never seen before")

The last two are the same move: take an abstract noun (structure, intent, a system) and have it perform an action like a character, so the sentence sounds like a tidy, quotable insight instead of a plain statement about what was actually built or decided. If a sentence reads like it could sit on a poster, rewrite it as a concrete claim about the actual work.

These read as a small, tidy insight dressed up in a stock turn of phrase, the sentence sounds resolved before it has actually said anything specific. Say the specific thing instead. When a new phrase gets flagged, add it here too.

## Tone

Senior product designer / design director. Direct, specific, no hedging, no over-explaining. Write in first person, as Katy, since she's the document's author, not as a description of her.

## Match content depth to the section's weight

Most sections in the GP document sit next to a screenshot, a FigJam board, or a research collage, and are not full case studies. For these sections:

- Don't describe what the image shows or narrate the diagram.
- Cover the intent instead: what she was trying to do, why, and how she went about it. The whys, whats, and hows of the process, not the specifics of the artifact.
- One tight paragraph is usually enough. If asked for a summary, compress to two lines without losing the reasoning.
- Save detailed, structural walkthroughs for sections she explicitly marks as a full case study.

**Worked example** (from the theming framework intro):

> Before touching any visuals, I wanted a clear read on where theming belonged in the product and why it was worth pursuing at all. I framed the problem first: which surfaces in the app could carry seasonal or cultural context without breaking usability, performance, or the existing brand system. Then I built the case for why that mattered, tying it back to engagement, differentiation, and how the platform reads to a user during moments that already carry weight for them.

Compressed to two lines:

> I framed where theming could live in the app without breaking usability or brand consistency, then built the case for why it mattered, tying it to engagement and how the platform reads during moments that already carry weight for a user.

## Grounding

Only draw on what's visible in the image, board, or what Katy has stated directly in the conversation. Never invent a number, name, mechanism, or detail that isn't there. If a needed detail is missing, leave it out rather than filling the gap.
